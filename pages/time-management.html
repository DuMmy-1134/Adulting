          Focused study techniques

          The Pomodoro Technique + –

          What it is: Developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, named after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he used as a student. You work in short, fixed bursts with a timer, then take a short break, and after a few bursts you take a longer break. Try it with the timer further down this page.

          How to do it:

          1. Pick one task.
          2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work on only that task until it rings.
          3. When it rings, stop — even mid-sentence — and take a 5-minute break away from your screen.
          4. Repeat. After 4 pomodoros (about 2 hours), take a longer break of 15–30 minutes.

          Why it works: A 25-minute block feels manageable even for a task you’re dreading, so it lowers the barrier to starting. The ticking clock creates just enough urgency to keep you off your phone, and the forced breaks stop you from burning out over a long study session. Counting your pomodoros also gives you a realistic sense of how long things actually take, which makes planning future sessions easier.

          Tips: Turn off notifications for the interval, not just “try to ignore them.” If you finish early, use the rest of the block to review or tidy up rather than starting something new. If something urgent interrupts you, it’s fine to end the pomodoro early — the technique is a tool, not a rule you have to suffer for.

          Timeboxing + –

          What it is: Giving a task a fixed, pre-decided block of time on your calendar or planner, and stopping (or deliberately deciding to extend) when that block ends — rather than working on something until it “feels” done.

          How to do it:

          1. List what you need to do for the day or week.
          2. Estimate a realistic amount of time for each item and put it directly onto your planner at a specific time, not just as a to-do item.
          3. Work on that task only during its box.
          4. When the box ends, stop. If it’s not finished, consciously decide whether it gets a new box later, rather than letting it silently run over.

          Why it works: It counters what’s sometimes called Parkinson’s Law — work tends to expand to fill however much time you give it. A tight, specific box creates urgency the same way a deadline does. It also makes your day visible: if you try to timebox 14 hours of tasks into a 10-hour day, you find out during planning, not at 11pm. This is exactly what the timetable tool on this page is built around — every task you add has a start time and a length, and the site warns you if two tasks would clash.

          Tips: Add a little buffer between boxes for things overrunning slightly. Timebox open-ended tasks too, like revision or research, which don’t have a natural finishing point and can otherwise eat a whole afternoon.

          The Two-Minute Rule + –

          What it is: If a task will take less than 2 minutes to do, do it immediately instead of writing it down to do later. It comes from David Allen’s “Getting Things Done” method.

          How to do it:

          1. When a small task comes up (replying to a message, filing one document, packing your bag for tomorrow), ask: would this take less than 2 minutes?
          2. If yes, do it right now, on the spot.
          3. If no, add it properly to your to-do list or timetable instead of trying to remember it.

          Why it works: Writing a tiny task down, filing it, and coming back to it later often takes more effort than just doing it. Left alone, small tasks pile up into a backlog that feels much bigger than it actually is, and they nag at your attention even when you’re not doing them (“open loops”). Clearing them immediately keeps your list focused on things that actually deserve planning.

          Worth knowing: You’ll sometimes see “the 2-minute rule” used differently in habit-building advice (from James Clear’s Atomic Habits) — scaling a new habit down to a version that takes under 2 minutes, e.g. “read every night” becomes “read one page.” Same name, different idea; both are useful, just for different situations.

          Eat the Frog First + –

          What it is: Do your single biggest, hardest, or most-avoided task of the day first, before anything else. The name comes from a line attributed to Mark Twain — “If it’s your job to eat a frog, it’s best to do it first thing in the morning” — popularised as a productivity method by Brian Tracy’s book Eat That Frog!

          How to do it:

          1. The night before (or first thing in the morning), decide what your one “frog” is — the task you’re most likely to put off.
          2. Do that task before checking messages, email, or anything easy and satisfying.
          3. If you have more than one big task, tackle the worst one first — “if you have to eat two frogs, eat the ugliest one first.”

          Why it works: Willpower and focus are usually highest earlier in the day and drop the more decisions you make (sometimes called decision fatigue). Avoiding a hard task all day doesn’t make it smaller, it just adds a background hum of stress and often leads to “productive procrastination” — staying busy with small, easy tasks to avoid the one that matters. Getting the frog out of the way early also gives you a real sense of progress that carries through the rest of the day.

          Tips: Decide on your frog in advance so you don’t waste your best hours deciding what to work on. If your frog is too big to finish in one sitting, it’s fine for “eating the frog” to mean starting it, not finishing it.
