Communication Skills

Master communication with simple, step-by-step guides for the conversations that actually matter.

Your communication toolkit

Good communication isn’t about big speeches — it’s a handful of small habits you can practice in everyday conversations. Pick a skill below and work through the tips inside.

Starting Conversations + –

Most awkward silences happen because one side is waiting for a perfect opening line. There isn’t one — a plain, low-stakes comment works just as well.

  • Comment on something you both already share — the class, the queue, the weather — before asking anything personal.
  • Ask open questions ("What made you pick this module?") instead of yes/no ones ("Do you like this module?").
  • Follow up on their answer with a related question rather than switching straight to your own story.
  • It’s fine to name the moment: "I don’t think we’ve met — I’m Alex" works better than waiting to be introduced.

Before / after

Don’t say: “Hey, how’s it going?”

Try instead: “This module’s been rough, right? Did you manage the reading last night?”

The first invites a one-word "fine" and dies there. The second references something specific and gives them an easy way to keep talking.

Active Listening + –

Most people listen while already planning what they’ll say next. Active listening means actually processing what the other person said before responding.

  • Reflect back the gist of what you heard ("So the deadline moved — that's why you're stressed?") before adding your own opinion.
  • Let a pause sit for a second instead of jumping in — people often add the most important part right after a short silence.
  • Ask a follow-up question about their point before pivoting to a story of your own.
  • Put your phone away or face-down — glancing at it mid-conversation reads as "you're not that interested," even if you are.
Handling Difficult Conversations + –

Conflict and sensitive topics go better when you plan the conversation instead of having it in the heat of the moment.

  • Use "I" statements instead of "you" statements — see the before/after below.
  • Pick a private moment instead of raising it in a group chat or in front of others.
  • State the issue, then stop talking — give the other person room to respond instead of listing every grievance at once.
  • It’s okay to say "let me think about this and get back to you" instead of responding while you’re still upset.

Before / after

Don’t say: “You always leave me out.”

Try instead: “I felt left out when the plans changed without me.”

The first sounds like an accusation and puts the other person on the defensive. The second states how you feel without blaming them, so they’re more likely to actually hear you.

Digital Communication Etiquette + –

Tone doesn’t travel well over text — a short reply can read as cold even when it isn’t. A little extra clarity goes a long way.

  • Re-read a message before sending it, especially anything written while annoyed.
  • If a group chat is getting heated, move it to a call or a 1:1 instead of typing it out publicly.
  • A one-line "seen, will reply properly tonight" beats leaving someone on read for two days.
  • Keep group chats on-topic — side conversations are easier in a separate thread or DM.

Before / after

Don’t send: “k.”

Try instead: “Got it, thank you! I’ll get started on this today.”

The first reads as cold or annoyed even if you didn’t mean it that way. A few extra words cost you nothing and remove all the guesswork for the other person.
